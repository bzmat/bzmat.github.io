By aggregating the sample means and sample variances across all attackers, I empirically validate the properties of unbiased estimators and discuss the effect of sample size on estimator variability in inferential statistics.

Ex. 1 – Welford's Recurrence Formulas

Derivation

Let Mk be the mean of the first k samples, and Sk be the sum of squared differences from the mean: Sk = ∑i=1k (xi - Mk)2.

Knuth's formula for the running mean is:
Mk = Mk-1 + (xk - Mk-1) / k

To derive the recurrence for Sk, we expand the squared differences for k terms, substituting Mk with its recursive definition. A well-known algebraic reduction leads to the elegant formula:
Sk = Sk-1 + (xk - Mk-1)(xk - Mk)

The sample variance is then simply the sum of squares divided by Bessel's correction factor:
s2 = Sk / (k - 1)

Code Implementation

The core logic implemented in JavaScript requires only a few lines to update the mean and the sum of squares (M2) iteratively:

let delta = x - mean;
mean += delta / k;
M2 += delta * (x - mean); 
// Sample Variance = M2 / (k - 1)

Statistical Conclusions

Based on the empirical results generated above, several fundamental principles of inferential statistics become evident:

  1. Unbiased Estimators: The average of the sample means closely approximates the theoretical mean (μ = 5). Similarly, the average of the sample variances (using Bessel's correction, divided by n-1) converges to the theoretical variance (σ2 ≈ 8.33). This proves that Welford's variance calculation provides an unbiased estimator of the population variance.
  2. Effect of Increasing n (Sample Size): As you increase the number of systems (n), the individual sample means computed by each hacker cluster much more tightly around 5.0. Mathematically, the variance of the sampling distribution of the mean is σ2 / n. A larger n directly reduces this variability, meaning any single hacker's average score becomes a highly reliable estimate of the system's true vulnerability.
  3. Usefulness in Inferential Statistics: In reality, we rarely know the theoretical μ and σ2. We only have one hacker's trajectory (one sample of size n). Because we know the sample mean and sample variance are unbiased, and because we know their variability shrinks as n grows, we can confidently construct Confidence Intervals to estimate the true parameters of the underlying population from a single limited sample.
