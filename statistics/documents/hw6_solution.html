1. Theoretical Concepts of Independence

In probability and statistics, two random variables X and Y are considered statistically independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other.

  • Joint Probability: The variables are independent if and only if their joint probability is the product of their marginal probabilities:
    P(X = x ∩ Y = y) = P(X = x) × P(Y = y) for all x, y.
  • Conditional Probability: Equivalently, the conditional probability of X given Y must equal the marginal probability of X:
    P(X = x | Y = y) = P(X = x).
  • Expected Frequencies (Eij): In a contingency table, if the variables are independent, the expected count for a cell in row i and column j is given by:
    Eij = (RowTotali × ColTotalj) / GrandTotal.

Statistical Association vs. Causality

If the Chi-Square test leads us to reject the null hypothesis of independence, we conclude that a statistical association (or dependence) exists between the variables. However, it is crucial to remember that correlation does not imply causation. An association merely indicates that the variables co-vary in a predictable manner. Causality requires further evidence (often experimental design) to prove that changes in X directly produce changes in Y, ruling out confounding variables or reverse causality.
